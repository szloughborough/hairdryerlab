## Why there is no decibel ranking to give you

**This is the section that most guides skip, and it is the reason this page reads differently.**

A sound pressure measurement is only meaningful with four things attached: **distance**, **weighting** (A-weighted is standard for perceived loudness), **background level**, and **the setting the device was on**. None of the four is standardised in this category.

**What that means numerically.** Sound pressure falls by roughly **6 dB for every doubling of distance**. A dryer measured at 10 cm would read about **20 dB higher** than the same dryer measured at 1 metre. That 20 dB gap is **larger than the difference between any two hair dryers** — so a figure without a distance is not just imprecise, it is uninterpretable.

**Why the industry has not fixed this.** There is no consumer-facing test standard for hair dryer noise, and there is no commercial incentive to create one. A brand claiming "only 68 dB" would have to define the conditions, and those conditions would then be comparable — which is exactly what the vaguer claim avoids.

**Our position.** We are building a measurement protocol that records sound pressure at **30 cm and at 1 metre**, against a recorded background level, with the same meter for every unit. Until that data exists for a model, our comparison tables show **"not yet tested"** rather than an estimate. See [how we test](/how-we-test/).

## What actually makes a hair dryer loud

**Three mechanisms, and only one of them is about the motor.**

**1. Motor speed and type.** Conventional dryers use brushed motors turning at roughly 15,000–20,000 rpm. High-speed brushless dryers turn far faster. A brushless motor eliminates the friction and electrical arcing of carbon brushes, which removes one noise source — but the much higher rotational speed can raise the fundamental frequency, and **pitch affects perceived annoyance separately from measured loudness**.

**We have not measured this on any model**, and we would be speculating if we told you which way it nets out. Both effects are real; which dominates is an empirical question nobody in this category has published an answer to.

**2. Air velocity at the outlet.** Noise scales with airflow speed. A dryer pushing air fast to shorten drying time is doing the thing that makes it loud. This is a genuine trade-off rather than a design flaw — and it is why **"quiet" and "fast drying" pull against each other**.

**3. Turbulence and the attachment.** Air moving through a grille, a concentrator or a diffuser generates turbulence, and turbulence is noise. Changing the attachment changes the sound. Some reviewers in our data describe the tone shifting noticeably between attachments *(buyer feedback)*.

**What is not a meaningful factor:** the colourway, the price, and the words "professional" or "salon". None of them appears in our noise complaint data in any pattern.

## A ten-minute method to compare dryer noise yourself

**Since no published figure exists, the comparison you can do yourself is more reliable than anything you can read.**

1. **Install a sound meter app** on your phone. Accuracy against a calibrated meter is poor, but *consistency* between two measurements on the same device is what you need
2. **Fix the distance at 30 cm.** Use a ruler or a marked string. Distance dominates everything else
3. **Measure the background first.** Note the room's level before switching anything on
4. **Use identical settings.** Same heat step, same speed step. Changing either invalidates the comparison
5. **Measure for 10 seconds and take the average**, not the peak — peak is dominated by the initial spin-up
6. **Test with the same attachment** on both dryers, and then again with the concentrator, since the attachment changes the sound
7. **Note the pitch, not just the number.** A dryer can measure the same and still be more annoying if its tone is higher

**What this gives you.** A ranking that is valid *for you, in your bathroom, on your settings* — which is more than any published comparison in this category can offer.

**What it does not give you.** A number you can compare against a stranger's review. Phone microphones vary, and so do rooms.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which hair dryer is the quietest?**
We cannot tell you, and neither can anyone else publishing a comparison without a sound meter. No brand in this category publishes a measured decibel figure at a stated distance, so there is no verified ranking to report. **Why do hair dryers not publish decibel ratings?**
There is no industry-standard test protocol for it — no agreed distance, no agreed weighting, no agreed background level. A figure measured 10 cm from the outlet and one measured at 1 metre would differ by around 20 dB, which is a larger gap than between any two dryers in this category.

**What actually makes a hair dryer loud?**

A high-speed brushless motor spins far faster than a conventional one, and pitch affects perceived annoyance independently of measured loudness. We have not measured any of these on any model.

**Is a high-speed brushless hair dryer quieter than a conventional one?**
We cannot confirm that either way, and we will not guess. The mechanism is plausible in both directions: brushless motors eliminate brush friction, but they spin far faster, which can raise pitch. Without measurement this is speculation, so we treat it as unverified.

**How can I compare hair dryer noise myself?**
